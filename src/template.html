Spectral Line Wiki

A working reference for precise spectroscopy: laboratory wavelengths of the neutral and singly-ionised atoms of hydrogen through einsteinium, the emission lines of the Earth's upper atmosphere (airglow), the solar and telluric Fraunhofer absorption lines, and every correction needed to put a measured wavelength into a physically meaningful rest frame — from the refractive index of air to the motion of the Local Group through the cosmic microwave background.

Conventions used throughout

  • Wavelengths in ångström (1 Å = 0.1 nm). Air wavelengths refer to standard air and are quoted above σ = 50 000 cm⁻¹, i.e. λvac ≥ 2000 Å (λair ≥ 1999.35 Å); below that only vacuum wavelengths are given (IAU convention, followed by NIST). Unlike NIST ASD’s default output, which returns to vacuum above 2 µm, this page keeps standard air in the infrared, as the Handbook does (beyond 1.69 µm all air formulas are extrapolations). Toggle Air / Vacuum in the top bar to change what the explorer, finder and sorting use; tables always show both.
  • Native values (as published by the source) are printed verbatim. Converted values carry as many decimals as the source supports; digits beyond the source precision are faded.
  • σλ is the source's stated uncertainty (or the bound of a source code, e.g. HITRAN) where one exists; otherwise it is implied as 2.5 units of the last published digit — the lower end of NIST’s guideline that unstated uncertainties are generally 2.5–25 units of the least significant figure. Grades: A ≤ 1 mÅ, B ≤ 5 mÅ, C ≤ 10 mÅ, D > 10 mÅ.
  • Element-line air↔vacuum conversions use Peck & Reeder (1972), the formula that reproduces NIST ASD's own air/vacuum pairs (§4.2); the converter in §7.2 compares all standard formulas.

3Why a precision of 10⁻³ Å?

A wavelength is a velocity in disguise. The requirement follows from three numbers that happen to coincide: the velocity equivalent of 1 mÅ in the optical (tens of m/s), the line-centroid precision of modern high-resolution spectrographs, and the size of the physical effects — Earth rotation, solar gravitational redshift, convective blueshift — that must be corrected for.

3.1Velocity equivalent

Δv=cΔλλ⇒1 mÅ≙59.96 m s⁻¹ at 5000 Å,246.6 m s⁻¹ at 1215.67 Å,13.6 m s⁻¹ at 2.2 µm

Velocity equivalent of fixed wavelength errors versus wavelength. A 0.01 Å catalogue value (the precision of 43 % of the lines in the NIST Handbook as published) is worth 0.6 km/s at 5000 Å — the same size as the solar gravitational redshift.

3.2What spectrographs actually resolve

For a resolving power R the resolution element is Δλ = λ/R (≈ 44 mÅ at 5000 Å for R = 115 000). Photon-noise-limited centroiding of a single unblended line reaches a small fraction of it, roughly σλ ≈ FWHM / (SNR·√Npix) Bouchy et al. 2001; a few thousand lines are then averaged to reach m/s radial velocities. Hence at R ≳ 50 000 and SNR ≈ 100 a single line is located to ≲ 1 mÅ, and laboratory wavelengths must be at least that good for line-by-line work (line identification, blending, wavelength solutions, abundances in crowded spectra, lab-to-sky comparisons).

7Wavelength calibration and reference frames

An observed wavelength is the product of a laboratory wavelength and a chain of Doppler and gravitational factors. Each link must be removed — multiplicatively — before velocities from different instruments, epochs or objects can be compared.

7.1The correction chain

{{CHAIN_FIG}}
(7.1)λobs, airλlab, vac=1nair(λ,T,P,e,xCO2)·(1+zsrc)(1+zgrav,src)(1+zcos)(1+zB)(1+z⊙→frame)

zB removes the observer's motion relative to the Solar-System barycentre (Earth rotation + orbit + relativistic terms, §7.4); z⊙→frame removes the barycentre's motion relative to the chosen frame (LSR, Galactic centre, Local Group or CMB; §7.6–7.7); zcos is the cosmological redshift and zsrc the source's peculiar/internal motion. Only products of (1+z) factors are exact; adding velocities is a first-order approximation whose error grows as v1v2/c (§7.8).

7.2Air ↔ vacuum

{{AIRVAC_TEXT}}

Converter

Ambient presets:

Differences between standard-air formulas, relative to Ciddor (1996). Between 3000 and 8000 Å the standard-air formulas agree to ≤ 0.15 mÅ, and to ≤ 0.65 mÅ over 2000 Å–2.5 µm (beyond 1.69 µm all are extrapolations). The FITS-WCS formula of Greisen et al. (2006, eq. 65) is omitted: it refers to 0 °C air and sits ≈ −77 mÅ below the others at 5000 Å.

The vacuum–air difference itself is ≈ 1.1–1.9 Å between 4000 and 7000 Å (≈ 83–85 km/s) — confusing media is a far larger error than any choice of formula.
