Precise radial velocities need time in two separate ways, and the two must not be confused:

  • The epoch of the correction. vB (§7.4) is evaluated at the moment the photons reach the telescope. The ephemeris is read in TDB and the Earth's orientation in UT1.
  • The time tag of the measurement. For orbit fits and timing, each RV is labelled with the time the light would have reached the SSB, BJDTDB. The site arrival time (JDUTC) should be quoted as well Eastman et al. 2010.

  • Leap seconds. TAI − UTC was 10 s on 1972-01-01; 27 leap seconds have been added since. The last was at the end of 2016, so TAI − UTC = 37 s from 2017 January 1. Bulletin C 72 (July 2026) announced no leap second at the end of December 2026 IERS 2026. The CGPM has decided that the maximum value of UT1 − UTC will be increased in, or before, 2035, which in practice ends leap seconds CGPM 2022. UTC is discontinuous, so JDUTC and BJDUTC cannot be differenced across a leap second.
  • UT1. Only Earth orientation uses UT1. Ignoring |UT1 − UTC| ≤ 0.9 s costs ≤ 3.1 cm/s in vB (derived from ω²a; ≈ 2 cm/s in practice). Without leap seconds this error grows with time, so UT1 − UTC must then always be supplied Wright & Eastman 2014.
  • TDB − TT is a periodic relativistic term (time dilation and gravitational redshift of a clock moving with the geocentre). Its dominant annual term has amplitude 1.657 ms Fairhead & Bretagnon 1990, Kaplan 2005, and the peak-to-peak range is 3.4 ms Eastman et al. 2010. The topocentric part is ≤ 2.2 µs (derived). The same physics gives the 4.65 m/s clock term of the velocity correction: LB c = 4.648 m/s (§7.4).
  • Constants LG (IAU 2000 B1.9), LB, TDB0 and T0 (IAU 2006 B3, T0 = 1977 Jan 1.0 TAI): Petit & Luzum 2010. TDB was redefined in 2006 to agree with the JPL ephemeris time argument Teph Eastman et al. 2010.

Practical limits on the time stamp

  • Exposure epoch. FITS DATE-OBS is normally the UTC start of the exposure. The correct epoch is the flux-weighted mean time (§7.4, eq. 7.4.5).
  • Clocks. Unless independently verified, header times should not be trusted to better than 0.25 s. An NTP-synchronised Linux machine is typically good to ≈ 50 ms Eastman et al. 2010.
  • Coordinates. A 1′ position error shifts BJD by up to |r| δθ/c = 0.15 s, and giving an object 10° from the field centre the field-centre BJD errs by up to 89 s (derived). Eastman et al. quote 0.28 s and "as large as 200 s", roughly the annual peak-to-peak range (0.27 s and 160 s for their RA offset at δ = 0, derived) Eastman et al. 2010.
  • Numerical precision. One float64 JD near 2.46×10⁶ resolves only 40 µs (derived), and arithmetic on full JDs loses more. Keep two-part or modified JDs.
  • Plane-wave approximation. Eq. (7.5.4) assumes the target is at infinity. For stars the error is ≤ 1 ms (Proxima Centauri); Solar-System targets need the exact light-time formula Eastman et al. 2010.

Consequences of timing errors for radial velocities

A time error δt enters in two places. It shifts the epoch at which vB is computed, which matters through the rates of §7.4: ≤ 3.4 cm/s per s from rotation plus ≤ 0.62 cm/s per s from the orbit. It also shifts the time tag of the RV, which matters through the orbit being fitted: δv ≤ 2πKδt/P for a semi-amplitude K and period P. All values below are derived.

Implementation in the calculator (§7.4)

  • UTC (ISO 8601) → TAI from the IERS leap-second table (TAI − UTC = 37 s from 2017-01-01; valid until the next announced leap second) → TT = TAI + 32.184 s → TDB with the leading Fairhead & Bretagnon terms (≈ 10 µs). UT1 = UTC + the entered UT1 − UTC.
  • The ephemeris is read at JDTDB and the Earth rotation angle at JDUT1, both at the entered (flux-weighted) epoch.
  • BJDTDB = JDTDB + ΔR⊙, with the plane-wave Rømer delay from the observer's own SSB position (geocentre + site). The Shapiro (≤ 0.11 ms) and topocentric Einstein (≤ 2.2 µs) terms are omitted, and the result is printed to 10⁻⁷ d (8.6 ms).
