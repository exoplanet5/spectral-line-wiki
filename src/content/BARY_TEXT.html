The barycentric correction zB converts a redshift measured at the telescope into the redshift that a fictitious observer at rest at the Solar-System barycentre (SSB) and at zero gravitational potential would measure — the reference observer of IAU 2000 Resolution C1 Lindegren & Dravins 2003. It is dominated by the Earth's orbital motion (±30 km/s) and rotation (≤ 0.47 km/s), and contains a nearly constant +4.65 m/s relativistic clock term. Two rules matter more than any formula: evaluate it at the flux-weighted epoch of the exposure, and apply it multiplicatively Wright & Eastman 2014.

At the 1 mÅ precision of this wiki the rotation term (7.8 mÅ) and the orbital term are resolved, while the lunar reflex, the solar reflex and all relativistic terms stay below one unit of the last digit. They matter for m/s-class radial velocities, where 1 m/s ≙ 1.67×10⁻⁵ Å at 5000 Å.

Earth rotation

The observer's rotational velocity component toward a target at hour angle H (positive west) and declination δ is

(7.4.1)vrot=−ωρcosφ′cosδsinH,ρcosφ′=(N+h)cosφ,N=a1−e2sin2φ
  • ω = 7.292115×10⁻⁵ rad/s and a = 6 378 137 m Petit & Luzum 2010 give ω a = 465.10 m/s at the equator (derived); ρ cos φ′ is the distance from the rotation axis (6006.2 km and 437.98 m/s at Mauna Kea). φ is the geodetic latitude and h the ellipsoidal height (WGS84).
  • Sign. vrot is the correction to add, in the convention of astropy and of eq. (7.4.3): positive for rising targets (H < 0, observer approaching), negative for setting targets. The observed RV of a setting star therefore contains +ω ρ cos φ′ cos δ sin H.
  • Rate. dvrot/dt = −ω² ρ cos φ′ cos δ cos H, largest at meridian transit, where vrot itself is zero: ≤ ω² a = 3.39 cm/s per second, i.e. ≤ 2.03 m/s per minute (derived; Wright & Eastman quote 3.4 cm/s/s).
  • H and δ refer to the true equator of date, so precession and nutation must be applied; precession matters already at 1 m/s and nutation at ≈ 3 cm/s Wright & Eastman 2014. Use the catalogue (BCRS) direction, not the aberrated apparent place.

Earth orbit, lunar reflex and the Sun's barycentric motion

  • The geocentre moves at 29.26–30.31 km/s (mean 29.78 km/s) relative to the SSB, and its acceleration is 5.70–6.17 mm/s² (derived, DE440s). The projected term has an annual semi-amplitude of ≈ 29.8 cos βecl km/s. It changes by ≤ 0.62 cm/s per second, ≤ 0.37 m/s per minute.
  • The geocentric vector already contains the Earth's monthly motion about the Earth–Moon barycentre, 11.7–13.4 m/s (mean 12.4 m/s). Codes that use the EMB instead of the geocentre miss this term.
  • The Sun moves 8.5–16.1 m/s about the SSB, mostly the 12.4 m/s reflex of Jupiter. A heliocentric correction therefore differs from the barycentric one by up to 16.1 m/s. Heliocentric corrections are usually Galilean (astropy's kind='heliocentric' is), so they also lack the 4.65 m/s clock term. In the worked example below, barycentric minus heliocentric is −4.22 m/s: −8.94 m/s from the Sun's motion plus +4.71 m/s of relativistic terms. IAU 2000 C1 specifies the barycentre.

Relativistic terms and the full correction

To first order in v/c and in the potentials, the correction is the observer's velocity toward the target plus a direction-independent clock term:

(7.4.2)czB≈(v→⊕+v→rot)·n^+v22c+∑iGMicdi,⟨v22c+∑iGMicdi⟩=LBc=4.648 m/s

The clock term is the same rate offset that separates TDB from TCB (§7.5): its DE440s mean, 4.648 m/s, equals LB c, of which LG c = 0.209 m/s is the Earth's own potential (derived). Its annual modulation by the orbital eccentricity, amplitude 2e GM⊙/(a c) ≈ 0.1 m/s Lindegren & Dravins 2003 or 0.20 m/s peak-to-peak (DE440s, derived), is what matters for RV time series; the mean matters for absolute RVs and for comparing stellar or solar lines with laboratory wavelengths. The exact form, Wright & Eastman (2014) eq. 28, is

(7.4.3)zB=γ⊕1+β→⊕·ρ^1+zGR1+β→s·r^01+β→s·ρ^−1−zLT−zSD,zGR=−∑iGMic2di
  • β⊕ is the observer's barycentric velocity / c (orbit + rotation) and γ⊕ its Lorentz factor. ρ̂ is the direction from the observer to the star, corrected for proper motion and parallax, and r̂0 the catalogue direction at the reference epoch. di is the distance from the observer to body i; at 1 cm/s only the Sun and the Earth matter.
  • The second fraction, with the star's systemic velocity βs, carries the secular acceleration (0.3 m/s per year for a star at 10 ly with μ = 2″/yr) and the parallax × proper-motion cross term. zLT (light-travel time of the moving star) and zSD (Shapiro delay, ≤ 1.5 mm/s for τ Ceti) are needed only below 1 cm/s Wright & Eastman 2014.
  • astropy's SkyCoord.radial_velocity_correction evaluates the first fraction with Sun, Jupiter, Moon and Earth in zGR. It adds the star term only for a SkyCoord with full space motion, and omits zLT and zSD Astropy Collaboration 2022.
Same symbol, different quantity. In Wright & Eastman (2014) and in astropy, zB is the correction (eq. 7.4.3). In IAU 2000 C1 and Lindegren & Dravins (2003), zB is the corrected barycentric redshift of the source, and c zB is the "barycentric radial-velocity measure", a spectroscopic quantity rather than a physical velocity.

Applying the correction

(7.4.4)1+ztrue=(1+zmeas)(1+zB)⇔λbary=λobs(1+zB)⇒vtrue=vmeas+vB+vmeasvBc
  • Here v ≡ c z in the optical convention, and vB = c zB is the value printed by the calculator and returned by astropy. Wright & Eastman's eqs. 10–11 stress that the correction is multiplicative, not additive.
  • Error of simply adding vB: the cross term vmeas vB/c. For most stars zmeas is dominated by −zB, so the term is ≈ −c β² ≈ −3 m/s (2.96 m/s for the mean orbital speed, derived) Wright & Eastman 2014. With vB = −16.5 km/s it is −0.55, −2.75 and −16.5 m/s for vmeas = 10, 50 and 300 km/s (derived).
  • Scale vacuum wavelengths (or frequencies) by (1 + zB). Scaling air wavelengths directly ignores the dispersion of air and, for |vB| = 30 km/s, errs by 0.4 m/s at 5000 Å and 1.3 m/s at 3000 Å (derived with Ciddor 1996).
  • Sunlight reflected by Solar-System bodies (the Moon, asteroids, the day sky) needs a different correction: the Sun→reflector and reflector→observer Doppler factors both enter, and eq. (7.4.3) does not apply.

Exposure timing

A spectrum integrates zB(t) over the exposure, weighted by the detected flux F(t). The correction to apply is the flux-weighted mean:

(7.4.5)z¯B=∫F(t)zB(t)dt∫F(t)dt≈zB(t¯F)+12z¨Bσt2,σt2=T212 for constant flux
  • Flux-weighted midpoint. An exposure meter behind the slit or fibre records F(t). Its photocentre is the right epoch. A geometric midpoint can be off by more than 250 ms in variable seeing or with guiding errors, and 250 ms is the tolerance for 1 cm/s Wright & Eastman 2014.
  • Timing errors (derived): a 1 s error gives ≤ 3.4 cm/s (rotation) + ≤ 0.6 cm/s (orbit), and a 1 min error gives ≤ 2.0 + 0.37 m/s. If a cloud covers half of a 30 min exposure, the photocentre moves by 7.5 min, worth up to 18 m/s.
  • Curvature. Even at the correct photocentre, evaluating zB once misses the second-order term, T² z̈B/24 for constant flux. For the rotation term c z̈ ≤ ω³ ρ cos φ′ cos δ |sin H| ≤ 2.47×10⁻⁶ m/s³, which gives ≤ 3.7 cm/s, 0.33 m/s and 1.3 m/s for T = 10, 30 and 60 min (derived). The typical size is 10 cm/s, and some realistic cases exceed 1 m/s, so the exposure-meter curve should be stored and used as photon weights Tronsgaard et al. 2019.
  • Chromatic weighting. Airmass-dependent slit or fibre losses make F(t), and hence the effective epoch, depend on wavelength. This causes errors of at least several cm/s, and at least four exposure-meter channels across 380–680 nm are needed for 10 cm/s Blackman et al. 2017. With EXPRES the measured effect reached up to ≈ 1 m/s Blackman et al. 2019.

What the calculator computes

Included

  • UTC → TAI (leap-second table; TAI − UTC = 37 s since 2017-01-01) → TT → TDB (leading terms of Fairhead & Bretagnon 1990, ≈ 10 µs). UT1 = UTC + the entered UT1 − UTC.
  • Geocentre position and velocity relative to the SSB, including the lunar reflex, from a Chebyshev fit to DE440s for 1990–2066.
  • Site: WGS84 geodetic to ITRS coordinates. GCRS orientation from the Earth rotation angle and the CIO-based matrix, with IAU 2006 precession and the largest IAU 2000A nutation terms; the truncation (≈ 0.1″) costs < 0.3 mm/s. vrot = ω × r.
  • zB = γ(1 + β·n̂)/(1 + zGR) − 1 with the Sun, Earth, Jupiter (fixed 5.2 au, ≤ 0.2 mm/s) and Moon potentials, i.e. eq. (7.4.3) with βs = 0, exactly as astropy implements it.
  • Diagnostics: the orbital, rotational and relativistic parts (their sum equals vB to ≲ 0.5 mm/s), the Galilean heliocentric correction, the Rømer delay, BJDTDB (§7.5), and a year-long curve of vB for the target.

Omitted

  • Shapiro delay zSD (1.5 mm/s for τ Ceti; ≤ 2.3 mm/s at ≥ 30° from the Sun) and light-travel term zLT.
  • Proper motion, parallax and the stellar space-motion factor; enter coordinates at the epoch of observation. For nearby high-velocity stars this omits up to 0.19 m/s (Kapteyn's star; derived).
  • Polar motion (< 1 mm/s). UT1 − UTC defaults to 0, which costs ≤ 3 cm/s.
  • Exposure integration and chromatic weighting: enter the flux-weighted mid-time.

Validation. Against astropy 7.2 radial_velocity_correction with DE440s, over 300 random targets, sites and epochs (1990–2030): 0.26 mm/s rms, 0.68 mm/s maximum. Ten further cases for 1995–2060, including polar and Arctic sites: 0.27 mm/s rms, 0.59 mm/s maximum.
