A measured line shift is a dimensionless ratio. Expressing it as a “velocity” requires a convention, and the three conventions in common use differ by ≈ v²/c. FITS WCS Paper III (Greisen et al. 2006) defines all three, with receding objects positive:

(7.3.1) optical · VOPT, ZOPTZ=cz=cλ−λ0λ0
(7.3.2) radio · VRADV=cν0−νν0=cλ−λ0λ=cz1+z
(7.3.3) relativistic · VELO, BETAv=cλ2−λ02λ2+λ02=c(1+z)2−1(1+z)2+1⇔1+z=1+β1−β

Eq. 7.3.3 is Greisen et al.'s “apparent radial velocity” (eqs. 14, 16). It equals the true radial velocity only when the transverse velocity is zero. In general (their eq. 2, after Lang 1974):

(7.3.4)λ=λ0c+vrc2−vr2−vt2

A transverse velocity of 100 km s⁻¹ adds ≈ 15 m s⁻¹ (Greisen et al.), or vt²/2c = 16.7 m s⁻¹ (derived). With vt = 0:

  • the radio velocity spans −∞ to +c;
  • the optical velocity spans −c to +∞;
  • the relativistic velocity equals vr.

Only the optical form is linear in λ, and only the radio form is linear in ν. This is why optical spectra are usually expressed in cz (or on WAVE/AWAV axes) and radio spectra in V.

Frame changes are true velocities

Moving between rest frames is a Lorentz boost, so the relativistic formula applies. Greisen et al. (2006, §10.1) give the example of a 26.108 km s⁻¹ topocentric → barycentric correction: “this correction between reference frames is a true velocity and thus the relativistic formula … should be used.” For a barycentre moving at β = V/c relative to the target frame, with θ the angle between that motion and the direction to the source measured in the barycentric frame (the catalogue direction), the frame calculator in §7.7 composes

(7.3.5)1+zframe=1+zbaryγ(1−βcosθ),γ=(1−β2)−1/2

The equivalent form (1 + zbary) γ (1 + β cos θ′) needs the angle θ′ measured in the target frame. Using the barycentric θ in that form errs by β² sin²θ, up to 0.46 km s⁻¹ for the CMB frame and 0.30 km s⁻¹ for a 300 km s⁻¹ Local Group apex (derived).

The barycentric correction of §7.4 is likewise applied multiplicatively: (1 + ztrue) = (1 + zmeas)(1 + zB). The cross term |vmeaszB| is 2.75 m s⁻¹ for vmeas = 50 km s⁻¹ and |c zB| = 16.5 km s⁻¹ (derived).

What a spectrum actually measures: IAU 2000 Resolution C1

IAU Resolution C1 (2000) notes that “the naive concept of radial velocity being equal to the time derivative of distance becomes ambiguous at accuracy levels around 100 m/s”. It therefore recommends reporting high-accuracy spectroscopic results as the barycentric radial-velocity measure (text reproduced in Lindegren & Dravins 2003):

(7.3.6) IAU 2000 C1czB,zB=λ−λ0λ0,c=299792458 m s⁻¹

The terms in the definition:

  • λ is the wavelength a hypothetical observer would see if at zero gravitational potential, located at, and at rest with respect to, the Solar-System barycentre. It is obtained by removing the observer's motion and the Solar-System gravitational shifts (§7.4).
  • λ0 is the rest-frame (laboratory) wavelength.
  • The epoch is the barycentric time of light arrival (§7.5).

czB is optical-convention in form, but it is “just a quantification of the spectroscopic shift, not of physical velocity”. The “B” refers to the Solar-System barycentre, not the CMB rest frame. To O(c⁻²), Lindegren & Dravins (eq. 43) give

(7.3.7)1+zB=(1+𝐤′𝐯*c)(1−Φ*c2−|𝐯*|22c2)−1(1+X)

Here k is the coordinate (BCRS) direction to the star, not the aberrated one. The factors on the right are:

  • the line-of-sight Doppler term k′v*/c;
  • the source's gravitational potential Φ*, which gives the gravitational redshift;
  • the transverse Doppler term |v*|²/2c²;
  • 1 + X, everything that cannot be separated: convective shifts, line asymmetries, pressure shifts, rotation and activity, and the cosmological redshift.

For a solar-type star, the gravitational redshift (+636 m s⁻¹) and the convective blueshift (≈ −400 m s⁻¹) sum to ≈ +0.2 km s⁻¹, and the sum varies by ~200 m s⁻¹ from line to line. Lindegren & Dravins conclude that radial-velocity standard stars are unlikely to be “true” to better than 0.5 (perhaps 0.3) km s⁻¹ without a physical model of the star.

Spectroscopic versus astrometric (and kinematic) radial velocity

Lindegren & Dravins (2003) separate three quantities that can be used interchangeably only at accuracies coarser than ≈ 100 m s⁻¹:

  • kinematic radial velocity vr = u′v* (their eq. 25): the component of the star's BCRS coordinate velocity along the barycentric direction u. This is the “textbook” radial velocity.
  • astrometric radial velocity ρ = dr*/dtB (eq. 29; IAU 2000 Resolution C2): the rate of change of barycentric coordinate distance with respect to barycentric time of light arrival. It is measured geometrically, for example from the secular change of parallax or with the moving-cluster method, and is related to the kinematic radial velocity by vr = ρ(1 − ρ/c)⁻¹ (eq. 34).
  • spectroscopic barycentric radial-velocity measure czB (eq. 7.3.6): directly observable and independent of the coordinate system, but it contains Φ*, vt and X. It can be converted to vr or ρ “only in special circumstances”, namely when Φ*, vt and X are known.
Symbol clash. In Lindegren & Dravins (2003) and IAU C1, zB is the source's redshift reduced to the barycentre. In Wright & Eastman (2014) and astropy, zB is the correction factor for the observer's motion, with (1 + zC1) = (1 + zmeas)(1 + zB,WE14). The calculator in §7.4 returns the latter, as vB = c zB.
Reporting recipe.
  • Quantity: publish z or czB, not a “velocity”.
  • Header: state the convention (VOPT/ZOPT, VRAD or VELO), SPECSYS = BARYCENT, SSYSOBS = TOPOCENT, and VELOSYS. Give the medium (WAVE or AWAV) with the air formula used (§7.2).
  • Rest wavelengths: name the laboratory source of λ0.
  • LSR, Galactic, Local Group or CMB frames: also give the solar-motion vector adopted (§7.6–7.7).
