A wavelength measured in air is shorter than the vacuum wavelength of the same line by the phase refractive index of air. In every modern formula, n is the index of standard air, evaluated at the vacuum wavenumber:

(7.2.1)λair=λvacn(σ),σ=104λvac[Å]µm−1

With Ciddor (1996) standard air (derived), λvac − λair is 0.647 Å at 2000 Å, 1.394 Å at 5000 Å, 1.813 Å at Hα (6564.6 Å vac), 2.741 Å at 1 µm and 5.459 Å at 2 µm. At 5000 Å that is (n − 1)c ≈ 83.6 km s⁻¹. A medium mix-up is therefore a much larger error than any choice of formula.

Key numbers. The modern standard-air formulas (Edlén 1953 and 1966, Peck & Reeder 1972, Birch & Downs 1994 = Morton 2000, Ciddor 1996) agree to ≤ 0.15 mÅ over 3000–8000 Å, ≤ 0.21 mÅ over 3000–10 000 Å and ≤ 0.65 mÅ over 2000–25 000 Å (maximum pairwise spread, derived). Any of them is adequate at 1 mÅ. At 0.1 mÅ the formula and its standard-air definition must be named. Three widely copied conventions are off by more than 1 mÅ: Greisen et al. (2006) eq. 65, the SDSS DR7 polynomial and the IRAF defaults.

What “standard air” means

All standard airs are at 15 °C and 101 325 Pa (760 Torr) and are dry. They differ in the CO₂ content, the temperature scale and, in one case, the temperature itself.

The medium convention

  • IAU and NIST: wavelengths are given in standard air longward of λair = 2000 Å (λvac = 2000.648 Å) and in vacuum below (Morton 1991). ASD does not display air wavelengths below 185 nm, the short end of the Peck & Reeder fit.
  • NIST ASD (Kramida et al. 2024) sets its default boundary by wavenumber (show_av=2):
    • vacuum for σ ≥ 50 000 cm⁻¹ (λ < 2000 Å);
    • air for 2000–20 000 Å;
    • vacuum again above 20 000 Å.
    ASD's own example: 200.0648 nm in vacuum is 200.0000 nm in standard air. With show_av=4, ASD returns air from 1850 Å upward, including above 2 µm; this was verified from the returned medium labels. The ASD import for this page converts the 1850–2000 Å air values back to vacuum (§4.2).
  • This page: standard air for λ ≥ 2000 Å at all wavelengths, including the near-IR, and vacuum below. Use the top-bar toggle to show vacuum everywhere.

The formula family

σ is the vacuum wavenumber in µm⁻¹ (σ² in µm⁻²). The coefficients below are those of the primary sources, and the converter (SpecCore.AIR) uses them unchanged.

(7.2.2) Edlén 1953 · IAU108(n−1)=6432.8+2949810146−σ2+2554041−σ2

Edlén 1953 is the IAU standard, adopted in 1957 and reaffirmed in 1991 (Morton 1991; Calabretta 2026). Morton (1991, eq. 3) writes it as n = 1 + 6.4328×10⁻⁵ + 2.94981×10⁻²/(146 − σ²) + 2.5540×10⁻⁴/(41 − σ²).

(7.2.3) Edlén 1966108(n−1)s=8342.13+2406030130−σ2+1599738.9−σ2

Edlén 1966 also gives corrections for ambient air. Temperature and pressure (eq. 12): (n−1)tp = (n−1)s · p[1 + p(0.817 − 0.0133 t)×10⁻⁶] / [720.775 (1 + 0.0036610 t)], with p in Torr and t in °C. Carbon dioxide: (n−1)x = [1 + 0.540(x − 0.0003)](n−1)s. Water vapour: ntpf − ntp = −f (5.722 − 0.0457 σ²)×10⁻⁸, with f in Torr.

(7.2.4) Peck & Reeder 1972 eq. 3 · NIST ASD108(n−1)=8060.51+2480990132.274−σ2+17455.739.32957−σ2

Peck & Reeder 1972 give two fits:

  • Eq. 3 (five parameters) extends the fit down to 0.185 µm. This is the formula NIST ASD uses (see below). Its coefficients come from a secondary source, but they reproduce all six eq.-3 values of P&R Table V to ≤ 0.045×10⁻⁸.
  • Eq. 2 (four parameters, 0.23–1.69 µm): 10⁸(n−1) = 5 791 817/(238.0185 − σ²) + 167 909/(57.362 − σ²). This is the version named “PeckReeder1972” in specutils and PyAstronomy.
(7.2.5) Birch & Downs 1994 = Morton 2000108(n−1)s=8342.54+2406147130−σ2+1599838.9−σ2

Birch & Downs 1994 corrected a sign error in the IPTS-48 → ITS-90 factor of their 1993 paper (Birch & Downs 1993). The superseded 1993 constants were 8343.05, 2 406 294 and 15 999.

  • The 1994 constants appear in the NIST EMToolBox, the CRC Handbook and VALD. Morton 2000 (eq. 8) writes them as n = 1 + 8.34254×10⁻⁵ + 2.406147×10⁻²/(130 − s²) + 1.5998×10⁻⁴/(38.9 − s²), with s = 10⁴/λvac[Å].
  • Stated uncertainty: 3σ = ±3×10⁻⁸ over 350–650 nm.
  • Temperature and pressure (1993 eq. 12): (n−1)tp = p(n−1)s[1 + 10⁻⁸(0.601 − 0.00972 t)p] / [96095.43 (1 + 0.0036610 t)], with p in Pa.
(7.2.6) Ciddor 1996 · standard air108(nas−1)=5792105238.0185−σ2+16791757.362−σ2,(naxs−1)=(nas−1)[1+0.534×10−6(xc−450)]

Ciddor 1996 is P&R eq. 2 rescaled by ≈ 1.0000497, to ITS-90 and to 450 ppm CO₂. The IAG recommended it in 1999, and SDSS (since DR9), the IDL astrolib and APOGEE use it. The full model handles any T, p, relative humidity and xc (CO₂, ppm):

(7.2.7) Ciddor 1996 · full modeln−1=ρaρaxs(naxs−1)+ρwρws(nws−1)
(7.2.8) Ciddor 1996 · water vapour108(nws−1)=1.022(295.235+2.6422σ2−0.032380σ4+0.004028σ6)

The terms of the full model:

  • Densities: ρa and ρw are the densities of the dry-air and water-vapour components of the actual air. ρaxs is dry air at 15 °C and 101 325 Pa; ρws is pure water vapour at 20 °C and 1333 Pa. All come from the BIPM moist-air equation.
  • Constants: Ma = 10⁻³[28.9635 + 12.011×10⁻⁶(xc − 400)] kg/mol, Mw = 0.018015 kg/mol and R = 8.314510 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. The compressibility Z and the saturation vapour pressure use Ciddor's App. A constants.
  • Validity: −40 to +100 °C, 80–120 kPa, 0–100 % RH and ≈ 300–1690 nm. Ciddor expects an overall uncertainty no better than 2–5×10⁻⁸ (1σ).
(7.2.9) Greisen et al. 2006 eq. 65 · FITS AWAVλ=n(λa)λa,n(λa)=1+10−6(287.6155+1.62887λa2+0.01360λa4),λa in µm (air)
Greisen et al. (2006) eq. 65 is not 15 °C standard air. It is the IUGG/IAG 1999 phase-refractivity formula for 0 °C, 1013.25 hPa, dry air with 375 ppm CO₂ (IUGG 1999), even though FITS Paper III calls it the index “of dry air at standard temperature and pressure”. Air wavelengths computed with it are lower by 26 mÅ at 2000 Å, 77 mÅ at 5000 Å (≈ 4.6 km s⁻¹) and 375 mÅ at 2.5 µm (derived), relative to 15 °C standard air (Ciddor 1996). Greisen et al. say it “suffices … when no more than 0.25 parts per million accuracy is required”. The specutils documentation reaches the same 0 °C conclusion.

ASD quotes the P&R relative uncertainty as 5×10⁻⁹ for λ > 400 nm, rising to ≈ 9×10⁻⁸ at 185 nm, and adds it in quadrature to its air wavelengths. For Na I D2, for example, ASD gives 5891.583264 ± 0.000015 Å in vacuum but 5889.95095 ± 0.00003 Å in air. The mean residual of the 35 988 pairs is 0.0001 mÅ (derived).

Across 3000–26 000 Å, ASD air values sit +0.04 to +0.32 mÅ above Ciddor air values for the same vacuum lines. By band: +0.054 mÅ (3000–5000 Å), +0.097 (5000–10 000), +0.183 (10 000–20 000) and +0.288 (20 000–26 000); derived. A line list must therefore not mix ASD air strings with Ciddor-converted air values in one column.

The inversion problem: air → vacuum

Because n is defined at the vacuum wavenumber, λvac = λair n(σvac) is implicit. The fixed-point iteration

(7.2.10)λvac(k+1)=λairn(104/λvac(k)),λvac(0)=λair

converges in 3–4 steps to 10⁻¹² Å, because each step multiplies the error by only λ|dn/dλ| ≪ 1. The converter below uses this iteration for every formula; in the reference implementation round trips close to ≤ 4×10⁻¹² Å. Two shortcuts are common:

  • One evaluation at λair. Evaluating n once at the air wavelength (specutils 'inversion', IRAF) leaves +0.098 mÅ at 2000 Å, +0.018 mÅ at 5000 Å and +0.004 mÅ at 2 µm. The reverse shortcut that Greisen et al. recommend, n(λvac) in eq. 65, leaves +0.078 mÅ at 2000 Å and +0.020 mÅ at 5000 Å. All values are derived. These errors are harmless at 1 mÅ, but not at 0.1 mÅ in the UV.
  • VALD's closed-form inverse, fitted by N. Piskunov to reproduce the exact inverse of Morton 2000 (≤ 1×10⁻⁵ mÅ over 2000–25 000 Å, derived):

Observatory air versus standard air

  • Converting a catalogue between vacuum and standard air is a convention. The weather at the telescope does not enter; only the formula and its standard-air definition do.
  • A spectrograph in ambient air disperses light in site air. At 5000 Å on a mountain, site air differs from standard air by ≈ 0.3–0.5 Å (+324 mÅ for the 2600 m case above). This difference is almost entirely a wavelength-independent scale factor. The arc lines and the stellar light pass through the same air, so a wavelength solution fitted to arc lines that carry catalogue (standard-air or vacuum) wavelengths absorbs it. For the 2600 m case, a cubic fit leaves ≤ 0.02 mÅ over 3800–6800 Å and ≤ 0.17 mÅ over 3600–10 000 Å (derived).
  • What the wavelength solution does not absorb is a change in the air between the calibration and the science exposure: 4.9 mÅ K⁻¹ and 1.4 mÅ hPa⁻¹ at 5000 Å (≈ 290 m s⁻¹ K⁻¹ and 83 m s⁻¹ hPa⁻¹). This is why precision radial-velocity spectrographs, such as APOGEE (Allende Prieto 2011) and the HARPS/ESPRESSO class, are operated in vacuum.
  • Instruments that measure wavelength absolutely in air (FTS referenced to a He–Ne laser, wavemeters, combs used in air) need n(T, p, RH, CO₂) along the actual beam, from eqs. 7.2.7–7.2.8; standard air does not apply.
  • Arc-lamp reference lists (Th–Ar, U–Ne, Fe) are mostly measured as vacuum wavenumbers with vacuum FTS. Convert them with the same formula the pipeline uses for the science lines.
Validation and two misprints. Every formula here was checked against published tables: 304 checks in total, against the source papers, the NIST EMToolBox, the CRC Handbook, NIST ASD, IDL, SDSS, live astropy.wcs output and specutils run from its wheel. Apart from the two entries below, Ciddor's Tables 1–3 are reproduced to ≤ 0.06×10⁻⁸. Two entries in Ciddor (1996) appear to be misprinted, in units of 10⁸(n − 1):
  • Table 3, 40 °C / 80 kPa row: printed as 19996.5. The printed Birch & Downs value (19883.6) and the printed difference (−12.9) imply 19896.5; this work computes 19896.49.
  • Table 2, row 2: printed as 27394.0. The row-to-row change of the Birch & Downs calculated column (+1.4) implies ≈ 27394.3; this work computes 27394.33. The printed value repeats the measured value of that row.
Extrapolation.
  • Above ≈ 1.69 µm. The dispersion formulas are empirical fits over ≈ 0.23–1.69 µm (P&R eq. 3 down to 0.185 µm), so every air wavelength beyond ≈ 1.69 µm, including all of the 2–2.5 µm values on this page, is an extrapolation. In the IR, water-vapour and CO₂ resonances also make the refractivity depend on humidity, which the standard-air formulas omit (Greisen et al. 2006; Mathar 2004). NIST ASD allows air output above 1690 nm “because the formula … behaves smoothly in this range”, but advises caution.
  • Below 2000 Å. The formulas diverge, and their longest-wavelength poles lie at 1320 Å (Ciddor, P&R eq. 2), 1562 Å (Edlén 1953), 1595 Å (P&R eq. 3) and 1603 Å (Edlén 1966). Between 1850 and 2000 Å, P&R eq. 2 and Ciddor already sit 1.6–2.9 mÅ from ASD. ASD itself warns that Ciddor's formula errs by −1.0×10⁻⁸ at 228 nm and −1.6×10⁻⁶ at 185 nm. Use vacuum wavelengths below 2000 Å.
