Airglow is the faint, non-thermal light of the Earth's upper atmosphere, from the mesopause region (≈ 75–105 km) and, for some atomic lines, the thermosphere above ≈ 200 km. Daytime solar UV photolysis and photoionisation produce radicals, ions and metastable atoms; their chemistry releases the stored energy as lines and bands, also through the night Noll et al. 2025. Below ≈ 2.3 µm it dominates the night-time emission of the atmosphere, and in the near-IR it can outshine scattered moonlight. Longward, thermal emission takes over. This section lists airglow lines out to the OH fundamental bands at 4.2 µm and leaves out the thermal-IR bands of NO, CO₂ and O₃.

Kinds of upper-atmosphere emission

  • Nightglow: chemiluminescence and recombination in darkness. Reference intensities on this page are the PALACE Cerro Paranal climatology (24.6° S; zenith, nocturnal annual mean, 10.7-cm solar flux 100 sfu).
  • Twilight glow: resonance scattering of sunlight by atoms and ions above the Earth's shadow. Typical values are Na D ≈ 3 kR, He I 10830 ≈ 1 kR (0.7–2.8 kR), K 30–37.5 R, Ca⁺ a few to ≈ 100 R and Li ≈ 20 R Hunten 1967.
  • Dayglow: photoelectron impact, photodissociation and resonance fluorescence. At the zenith, [O I] 6300 is 2–60 kR, Na D 5–40 kR, O₂ b–X ≈ 300 kR and O₂ a–X ≈ 25 MR Noxon 1968. Viewed in the UV at nadir from 600 km, H Lyα is 35 kR and O I 1304 is 10.5 kR Meier 1991.
  • Aurora: the same species, excited by precipitating electrons. Brightness classes IBC I–IV correspond to 1, 10, 100 and 1000 kR of [O I] 5577 Ivănescu et al. 2021, compared with 163 R for the Paranal nightglow. The mean auroral peak heights are 114.8 km for 5577 and 116.6 km for N₂⁺ 4278 Whiter et al. 2023.
  • Geocorona: solar Lyα and Lyβ resonance-scattered by exospheric hydrogen, Balmer emission excited by solar Lyβ fluorescence, and He II 304 from the plasmasphere. Its intensity depends on the shadow height, not on a fixed layer.
  • Meteors: transient emission from ablated metal atoms and ions near 100 km Moorhead et al. 2019. The spectrum has a main component at 3500–4700 K and a ≈ 10 000 K component (Mg II, Si II, Fe II) Borovička 1993.

Intensities are column emission rates in rayleigh. 1 R = 10⁶/4π photons cm⁻² s⁻¹ sr⁻¹. Values vary by factors of 2 or more with local time, season, latitude and solar activity, so each value on this page carries its viewing condition.

Excitation mechanisms

  • Chemiluminescence (81–92 km): H + O₃ → OH*(v ≤ 9) + O₂ (Meinel bands); H + O₂ + M → HO₂* + M; Fe + O₃ → FeO* + O₂; and the Chapman cycle Na + O₃ → NaO + O₂, NaO + O → Na(²P) + O₂, with an analogous cycle for K.
  • Barth mechanism (three-body O recombination, 94–97 km): O + O + M → O₂* + M populates the O₂ b, A, A′ and c states (Atmospheric, Herzberg and Chamberlain bands); then O₂* + O → O₂ + O(¹S) gives [O I] 5577.
  • Dissociative recombination (F region, ≈ 250 km): O⁺ + O₂ → O₂⁺ + O, then O₂⁺ + e⁻ → O(¹D) + O gives [O I] 6300/6364; NO⁺ + e⁻ → N(²D) + O gives [N I] 5198/5200.
  • Radiative recombination O⁺ + e⁻ → O* (∝ ne², peaking near the F-layer maximum): the nightglow O I 7774, 8446, 1356 and 1304 multiplets.
  • Resonance scattering and fluorescence (twilight, day): Na, K, Li, Ca⁺; He I 10830 from metastable He(2³S); geocoronal H; and Bowen fluorescence of O I 1027 → 8446 → 1304, pumped by solar Lyβ.
  • Photoelectron and particle impact (day, aurora): N₂⁺ First Negative (3914, 4278), the N₂ 1P, 2P, Vegard–Kaplan and LBH bands, O I 1356, and the auroral green line, which N₂(A) energy transfer also feeds.

OH dominates from the visible to 2.3 µm, with the brightest bands at 1.5–1.8 µm. Next come the O₂ bands at 0.762, 0.865, 1.27 and 1.58 µm, then the HO₂ near-IR pseudo-continuum. The atomic lines that matter lie mainly in 5000–8000 Å: [O I] 5577, 6300 and 6364, the O I 7774 recombination triplet, Na D and [N I] 5200. Together they contribute only ≈ 0.4 kR but are bright and isolated. In the 3000–9000 Å PALACE list, the ground-level line intensities sum to 5.0 kR for OH, 0.49 kR for O₂, 0.34 kR for O I and 35 R for Na (derived from the PALACE list).

Why airglow matters for wavelength calibration

  • Sky subtraction. Every ground-based spectrum carries the airglow. How well bright OH lines subtract depends on the accuracy of the wavelength solution and of the assumed line positions, including unresolved OH Λ doublets and faint blends. A complete model list such as PALACE (§5.3) includes the faint lines that observed atlases miss.
  • In-situ wavelength reference. Sky lines share the slit or fibre, the light path and the exposure of the science target. The Keck/HIRES atlas was compiled explicitly for echelle wavelength calibration Osterbrock et al. 1996. With the 58 UVES lines in 7000–7100 Å, a statistical calibration rms of ≈ 2 mÅ (85 m s⁻¹) is reachable Hanuschik 2003, but only with laboratory positions: the atlas positions there lie ≈ 15 mÅ high (next item).
  • Topocentric frame. Airglow is emitted 80–300 km above the telescope and, apart from atmospheric winds, is at rest in the topocentric frame. Sky lines must never be barycentre-corrected. They test the instrument's zero point and drift, not the barycentric chain of §7.4.
  • Systematic offsets. Observed atlas positions inherit the calibration of the spectrograph that measured them. In the red settings, UVES sky-line positions lie 7–15 mÅ (≈ 0.35–0.5 km s⁻¹, derived) above the laboratory/HITRAN positions, and UVES − NIST = +13 ± 2 mÅ for 13 atomic lines Cosby et al. 2006. Calibrate on laboratory or model positions, never on observed ones (§5.3).
  • Medium and formula. The model lists (PALACE, HITRAN) are native in vacuum; the UVES and Keck/HIRES atlases are native in air. PALACE converts with Edlén 1966, NIST ASD with Peck & Reeder 1972 and the Keck/HIRES list with Edlén 1953. The λair columns of the PALACE and near-IR tables are derived here with Peck & Reeder 1972; the “model λair” column of the UVES table uses Edlén 1966. These formulas, and Morton 2000, agree to ≤ 0.23 mÅ over 0.3–1.05 µm; Edlén 1966, Peck & Reeder and Morton agree to ≤ 0.5 mÅ out to 2.6 µm (derived; §7.2). The OH Δv = 1 lines at 2.5–4.2 µm lie beyond the fitted range of every formula, and there Peck & Reeder − Morton grows to 0.77 mÅ (derived), so use vacuum. Confusing air with vacuum costs 1.548 Å at the green line.
  • Intrinsic widths. The thermal Doppler FWHM is 13.8 mÅ for [O I] 5577 and 12.1 mÅ for Na D2 at 190 K, and 35.7 mÅ for [O I] 6300 at 1000 K (derived with the PALACE layer temperatures). Resolving the green line would need R ≈ 4 × 10⁵ (derived). A 1 mÅ centroid (54 m s⁻¹ at 5577 Å) is ≈ 7 % of the intrinsic width: attainable, but sensitive to faint OH and O₂ blends.
For mÅ work, take airglow positions from laboratory and model data: NIST ASD for atoms (§5.1), HITRAN for OH and O₂ (§5.3–5.4), and the Abrams et al. 1994 FTS values behind the Keck/HIRES list. Use observed sky atlases for identification, intensities and blending only.
