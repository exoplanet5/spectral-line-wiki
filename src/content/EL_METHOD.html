Primary source

Sansonetti & Martin 2005: J. E. Sansonetti & W. C. Martin, Handbook of Basic Atomic Spectroscopic Data, J. Phys. Chem. Ref. Data 34, 1559 (2005), NIST e-book edition (local copy, 911 HTML pages). Parsed: 99 elements, 193 spectra, 11 968 strong lines (1 054 quoted in vacuum below 2000 Å, 10 914 in standard air above), 2 401 persistent lines with energy levels, configurations, terms, J and transition probabilities. The e-book prints 12 008 strong-line rows: 12 Pt lines printed twice are kept once, and the 28 lines printed without an ionization stage (Os 5, Po 1, Es 22) are left out of the table because they cannot be assigned to a spectrum or matched to ASD (they are kept in the parsed data). Ground-state configurations are read from the e-book's explicit superscript markup, so occupancies are never guessed. One printed configuration is internally inconsistent: Dy II is printed 4f9(5I8)6s (64 electrons) and is given here as 4f10(5I8)6s, as in NIST ASD. Intensities are relative and source-dependent (mostly from Reader et al. 1980, who for about half the elements took them at least partly from the dc-arc measurements of Meggers, Corliss & Scribner 1975) and are set to 1000 for the strongest line of each spectrum — they are not basic data. An asterisk on an intensity marks lines too close to be measured separately.

The Handbook gives no wavelength uncertainties; its precision is implied by the number of decimals. Its introduction notes that the 3-decimal values taken from Reader et al. (1980) have stated uncertainties below 0.001 Å, that many 2-decimal values are rounded from 3-place literature values, and refers to the original papers for uncertainties. (Its warning of 1 to ~30 units of the last digit refers to the energy levels.)

Uncertainty rule used on this page. Where no uncertainty is stated, the implied uncertainty is 2.5 units of the last published digit. NIST ASD: “If such information is not available, the implied uncertainty is defined by the number of significant figures given: it is generally between 2.5 and 25 units of the least significant figure” Kramida et al. 2024. The rule takes the lower end of that range, so implied values are optimistic. Uncertainties stated by ASD are used as given. All uncertainties are stored to two significant figures. Upgrade with the NIST Atomic Spectra Database

Every spectrum (192 of 193; ASD has no Np II lines) was queried from NIST ASD Kramida et al. 2024 over its Handbook wavelength span on 2026-11-23. The queries returned observed and Ritz wavelengths with uncertainties, vacuum wavenumbers, configurations, terms, J, energies, Aki and accuracy codes, and bibliographic keys. ASD appends “+” or “*” to Ritz values computed online; these marks are not counted as digits. Each Handbook line was matched to ASD lines of the same spectrum within 5 units of its last digit or 20 mÅ. The nearest one was taken; near-ties (within half a unit, at most 0.5 mÅ) go to the allowed (E1) line, then the largest Aki, then the ASD intensity:

  • exact (9 541): equal within the rounding of the coarser value;
  • close (1 649): within 3 units of the coarser last digit or 3 mÅ — same line, re-measured or re-derived;
  • loose-unique (267): farther, but the only ASD line in the window, with a stated uncertainty ≤ 1 Handbook unit — ASD value adopted (Cr I 93, V I 44, Ti II 37, V II 26, Ne II 13, Hg I 12 …);
  • loose (191): tentative cross-reference only, Handbook value kept;
  • none (320).

Adoption rule for exact and close matches: only ASD values within 3 Handbook units (at least 3 mÅ) of the Handbook value count. Among them the value with the smallest stated uncertainty is adopted (observed or Ritz), but only if it has at least the Handbook's decimals or its uncertainty is ≤ 3 Handbook units. Otherwise an ASD value without stated uncertainty is adopted only if it has more decimals than the Handbook. Otherwise the Handbook value is kept. Result: 4 752 lines from ASD Ritz values, 1 750 from ASD observed values, 5 466 Handbook values kept.

Unresolved multiplets (118 lines, marked “mult”). Some Handbook lines are a blend of fine-structure components of one multiplet: He I triplets (23 lines), H I and He II n–n′ groups (25), and lines of Mg, Li, Be, Na, O, N and other light spectra. Components the Handbook lists as separate lines are not counted. A line is treated as a blend in three cases: ASD gives one observed value for several components (57); the Handbook value matches the gA-weighted centroid rather than a single component, or cannot tell the two apart (45); or it is a 2-decimal H I/He II value (16). Such a line never takes a single component's Ritz value. It takes ASD's observed blend value (43) or composite H I n–n′ entry (4) under the same rule, or keeps the Handbook value (71). The transition shown is the composite entry (H I, He II) or the strongest component.

Result. Uncertainty ≤ 1 mÅ (grade A): 4 241 lines (35 %), 3 060 of them with a stated ASD uncertainty. ≤ 5 mÅ (B): 4 562 (38 %), 3 599 of them 3-decimal values with the implied 2.5 mÅ. ≤ 10 mÅ (C): 300 (3 %), all stated by ASD. Above 10 mÅ (D): 2 865 (24 %). Of these, 2 527 remain at 0.01 Å or coarser with no finer value in ASD: mostly lanthanides, actinides and heavy transition metals (Nb I, Os I, Gd I/II, Re I, Xe II, Pm, Ho, Pa …). The other 338 have stated ASD uncertainties above 10 mÅ. In 302 cases the two sources differ by more than twice their combined uncertainty (62 exact, 78 close, 162 loose-unique); these are flagged ⚠ in the table. For unresolved multiplets that keep the Handbook value there is no single ASD value to compare, so they are not flagged.

Hg I: lamp type matters at the ~7 mÅ level. The Handbook's Hg I values are natural-Hg measurements (Burns, Adams & Longwell 1950). They differ from the ASD values adopted here by +3.9 to +14.8 mÅ (12 loose-unique lines, mean +6.8 mÅ). The Handbook's own Hg note says these values should not be used to calibrate pencil-type lamps if uncertainties below 0.01 Å are wanted: pencil-lamp wavelengths are longer by 0.0068(32) Å on average Sansonetti et al. 1996. Use values that match the lamp.

Which air/vacuum formula does ASD use?

A first check compares ASD air Ritz wavelengths with ASD vacuum wavenumbers for the matched lines (1 421 lines with ≥ 4-decimal Ritz values and σ < 0.2 mÅ). It is limited by the 3–4-decimal rounding of the wavenumbers (0.1–1 mÅ). Even so, its band medians from 2000 to 15 000 Å stay within ±0.05 mÅ for Peck & Reeder (1972) and Edlén (1966), whereas Ciddor (1996) leaves +0.02 to +0.13 mÅ and Birch & Downs (1994) +0.03 to +0.16 mÅ. The decisive test, on 35 988 ASD vacuum/air line pairs (1850–26 000 Å, §7.2), identifies the five-parameter Peck & Reeder eq. 3 Peck & Reeder 1972: 99.99 % of pairs agree within rounding. All vacuum↔air conversions of element lines on this page therefore use Peck & Reeder (1972) eq. 3, consistent with ASD.

ASD pitfall found while building this table: the ASD output option used for the main queries (show_av=4, “vacuum < 1850 Å, air > 1850 Å”) switches to air at 1850 Å, not 2000 Å as the default option does. The Handbook quotes vacuum up to 2000 Å, so matching without care would mis-pair its 133 vacuum lines in 1850–2000 Å by ≈ 0.6 Å. For the spectra concerned ASD was queried a second time in vacuum (show_av=3) over 1848–2002 Å, and ASD's own vacuum strings are used: 98 exact, 27 close, 3 loose, 5 none. Converting ASD's rounded air strings back to vacuum instead would have changed the last digit of 23 of the 128 matched values.

Known limitation: the matching is done line by line, not one-to-one. For 7 ASD lines, two close Handbook lines point to the same ASD line (e.g. Li I 6103.654 and 6103.667).
